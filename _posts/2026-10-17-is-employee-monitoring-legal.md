## The Short Answer: Yes, With Conditions

In the United States, employee monitoring is generally legal when an employer monitors company-owned equipment for legitimate business reasons—productivity, security, compliance, or protecting company assets. At the federal level, the Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA) permits monitoring of business communications on employer systems, and phone recordings are allowed under the one-party consent rule in many states.

But federal law is only the floor. As of March 2026, 20 US states have enacted comprehensive privacy laws that impact workplace privacy and employee monitoring. That patchwork is where most employers get tripped up, because what satisfies one state's rules won't necessarily satisfy another's.

## Why the Legal Landscape Changed in 2026

A wave of new legislation has reshaped the compliance picture. A few developments stand out:

- **Maine** passed one of the strictest laws in the country. A January 2026 law prohibits tracking on personal devices and requires annual written notice of all monitoring systems.
- **Connecticut** expanded its long-standing rules. Effective October 1, 2026, covered employers must disclose not only the types of electronic monitoring that may occur, but also the specific locations on the employer's premises where monitoring may occur.
- **New York** stacks multiple obligations. It requires written notice before monitoring begins, a signed employee acknowledgment, and a conspicuously posted physical notice in the workplace—three separate, mandatory requirements.

The takeaway: the states aren't harmonized—what satisfies New York doesn't automatically satisfy Connecticut. ## Notice vs. Consent: Know the Difference

One of the most common and costly mistakes employers make is treating "notice" and "consent" as the same thing. They aren't.

**Notice** means telling employees that monitoring happens. **Consent** means getting their explicit, often written, agreement to it. Conflating notice with consent—or applying one state's standard across the board—is exactly how compliant intentions turn into legal exposure.

Some states require only notice. Others require consent for specific functions—especially anything touching audio, biometrics, or personal devices. California and Illinois laws require employers to obtain consent from third parties before accessing employees' emails. When in doubt, the safest posture is to provide clear written notice *and* obtain a signed acknowledgment before any monitoring begins.

## Company Devices vs. BYOD: A Critical Distinction

The legal ground shifts dramatically depending on who owns the device. Monitoring a company-issued laptop for productivity or security is well within an employer's rights in most jurisdictions. Monitoring an employee's personal phone is a different matter entirely.

If you allow Bring Your Own Device (BYOD), tread carefully. The best practice is clear: if BYOD monitoring is genuinely necessary, get separate, explicit, written consent limited to work applications only—don't fold it into your general monitoring notice. ## What You Can Legally Monitor

When done transparently and on company equipment, most monitoring activities are permissible. These typically include:

- **Screen activity and application usage** — seeing which tools and programs employees use during work hours.
- **Website and URL visits** — a core feature of any modern **Time Tracker with URL Tracking**, which logs the sites visited on company systems to protect against security risks and distractions.
- **Time and attendance** — automated clock-in/clock-out, idle time, and project-based hours through **Employee Time Tracking Software**.
- **Email and file activity** on company accounts and devices, within the limits of applicable state law.
- **Productivity analytics** — aggregated reports on active versus idle time, apps, and workflows.

The common thread is legitimacy and transparency. Monitoring for a genuine business purpose, on equipment you own, after you've notified the team, sits firmly on the right side of the law in most regions.

## What Can Get You Into Trouble

Some practices invite legal risk even where general monitoring is allowed:

- **Secret or covert surveillance** in states that require notice or consent.
- **Tracking personal devices** without explicit, narrowly scoped consent—now outright restricted in states like Maine.
- **Recording audio or video** without the consent required under two-party-consent and biometric laws.
- **Monitoring during breaks or off-hours**, which many jurisdictions treat as off-limits.
- **Capturing biometric data** (keystroke biometrics, facial recognition) without specific biometric consent.

## Global Rules: GDPR and Beyond

If you employ people in the EU or UK, the bar is higher. Under the GDPR, monitoring must be lawful, proportionate, and transparent. Employers generally need a legitimate interest that doesn't override the employee's privacy rights, must conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment for intrusive monitoring, and must minimize the data they collect. "We monitor everything because we can" is not a defensible position under European law—and increasingly not under new US state laws either.

## How to Build a Compliant Monitoring Program

You don't need a law degree to get this right. A defensible program comes down to a repeatable framework:

1. **Define a legitimate purpose.** Document *why* you monitor—security, productivity, compliance, client confidentiality. Vague justifications don't hold up.
2. **Monitor company devices, scope BYOD narrowly.** Default to company-owned equipment. If personal devices are involved, get separate written consent limited to work apps.
3. **Provide clear written notice before monitoring starts.** A buried line in the handbook is no longer enough. Spell out what you track, where, and when.
4. **Collect signed acknowledgments.** Several states now require them, and they double as your legal basis for monitoring.
5. **Re-notify where required.** States like Maine mandate annual written notice. Build a reminder into your calendar.
6. **Minimize and secure the data.** Only collect what you need, restrict who can see it, and set retention limits.
7. **Be transparent with your team.** Monitoring employees secretly, even where legal, erodes trust. Openness protects both your people and your company.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is employee monitoring legal in the United States?

Yes. Employee monitoring is generally legal in the United States when organizations notify employees appropriately and monitor for legitimate business purposes such as productivity, compliance, or cybersecurity. However, privacy requirements vary by state, especially regarding personal devices and off-hours monitoring.

### Do I need employee consent to monitor?

It depends on your state and what you're monitoring. Many states require only notice for basic electronic monitoring, but functions like audio recording, biometrics, and personal-device tracking often require explicit consent.

### Can I monitor employees working from home?

Yes, on company-owned devices and with proper notice. Remote work doesn't remove monitoring rights, but personal-device and off-hours restrictions still apply.
